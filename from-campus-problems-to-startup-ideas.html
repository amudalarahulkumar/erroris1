From Campus Problems to Startup Ideas

How everyday student challenges can inspire useful businesses.

E-Cell Blog Series Student Innovation Learning & Growth
Observe · Understand · Test A familiar problem. A fresh possibility.

A long queue at the printing shop. Useful textbooks lying unused. Students struggling to find teammates with the right skills. These may seem like ordinary campus inconveniences, but they contain a valuable starting point: a real problem experienced by real people.

You do not need an extraordinary invention to begin thinking like an entrepreneur. Start by paying closer attention to the places where people struggle. A campus is full of shared routines, limited resources, and unmet needs. The challenge is to find a problem worth solving, rather than simply an idea that sounds impressive.

1. Notice What Students Work Around

Look for repeated frustrations. Where do students make unnecessary trips, spend extra money, or depend on confusing information? Notice the workarounds too: crowded group chats, handwritten lists, repeated phone calls, or asking several friends for the same answer.

A workaround shows that someone already cares enough to act. For example, students repeatedly sharing photos of second-hand textbooks may suggest a need for an easier way to match buyers and sellers. It is a clue to investigate, not proof that a business will succeed.

The best place to look for an idea is often the problem people have learned to live with.

2. Understand the Problem Before Choosing a Solution

Talk to the people who experience the issue. Instead of asking, “Would you use my app?”, ask them to describe what happened the last time they faced it. What did they do? What was difficult? What did their current solution cost them?

Listen for specific experiences. A student saying “That sounds nice” offers less useful evidence than a student explaining how they visited three shops to find a particular book. Ask permission before recording responses and collect only the information you need.

Write a clear problem statement: “Students who need used course books struggle to find available copies because listings are scattered across different groups.” This identifies the user, the difficulty, and a possible cause without assuming an app is the answer.

3. Explore Possibilities Around You

The examples below are hypothetical starting points. Each needs user research, a practical test, and relevant campus permission before it becomes a real initiative.

Unused books and equipment

A simple resale directory could connect students who need an item with those who no longer use it. Test whether listings lead to completed exchanges.

Printing-shop queues

With a shop’s cooperation, an organised order-and-collection process could reduce confusion. Test whether orders remain accurate and students find collection easier.

Finding project teammates

A small directory of interests and skills could help students form suitable teams. Test whether introductions result in useful collaborations.

Not every campus problem needs a startup. Some are better handled by a student club, a clearer college process, or an existing service. Choosing the right approach is part of good entrepreneurial thinking.

4. Test the Smallest Useful Version

Once you understand the need, create the simplest version that can deliver value. A form, a spreadsheet, a basic webpage, or a manually coordinated service may be enough. Building a full product before testing demand can hide important questions beneath unnecessary features.

For a used-book idea, start with one course or department. Collect listings with the owners’ consent, make availability clear, and help interested buyers contact sellers. Observe whether people actually complete exchanges and what makes the process difficult.

Set a clear test goal: completed exchanges, successful collections, or useful team matches. Page visits and compliments can show interest, but they do not establish that a solution works.

5. Ask Whether the Idea Can Sustain Itself

A useful service needs a workable way to continue. Consider the effort involved, operating costs, who benefits, and who might pay. For a resale service, for example, students may appreciate free listings but reject a fee. A shop partnership may suit a printing service better than charging students directly.

Be transparent about any charges. Check relevant college rules, protect personal information, and avoid promises you cannot fulfil. If delivering the service costs more than it can reasonably earn, simplify the model or consider running it as a community initiative.

6. Use Your E-Cell as a Place to Learn

An E-Cell can create space for students to discuss problems, challenge assumptions, and practise presenting ideas. Seek feedback from peers and mentors, and look for teammates whose skills complement yours.

For Team Erroris, this approach offers a practical way to encourage entrepreneurial thinking: observe campus needs, discuss them openly, and learn through small experiments. A pitch becomes stronger when it explains what users experienced and what a test revealed.

Your Starting Point Is Closer Than You Think

Choose one campus frustration you understand. Speak to a few people who face it, describe the problem clearly, and test one small improvement. Use what you learn to decide whether to improve the idea, change direction, or stop.

A campus problem becomes a startup opportunity when you can solve it in a way people value—and find a practical way to keep delivering that value.

Notice a problem. Start a conversation.

Your next idea could begin with an everyday campus challenge.
